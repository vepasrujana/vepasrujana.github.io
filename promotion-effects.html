Executive summary

Using IRI household scanner data, we modeled 3 separate shopper decisions for liquid laundry detergent: whether to buy at all, which of 4 brands to buy (Wisk, All, Tide, Cheer), and how much to buy. We then simulated how price cuts affect gross profit for the retailer and the manufacturers. In-store displays were the strongest lever for brand choice, raising choice odds by 270% vs. 104% for price cuts, and they lifted expected volume 2.9-4.2x. Price cuts were profitable for both sides when the retailer passed the full discount to shoppers. When it passed on only 70%, the retailer's gains grew to as much as 60%, while the manufacturers of Tide and All lost profit. We recommended full pass-through for high-demand brands, at least 70% for lower-demand brands, and prioritizing displays over deep discounts.

Objective

Measure how price cuts, displays and feature ads affect each stage of the purchase decision, and assess the profit impact of price cuts for the retailer and the manufacturers.

Data and approach

The data covered 177 households across 4 stores over 135 weeks: 19,157 household-weeks and 781 purchase occasions. We built a 3-stage model of purchase behavior:

  1. A binary logit for whether a household buys in the category, selected using BIC.
  2. A multinomial logit for which brand it chooses.
  3. 4 brand-level semi-log models for how much it buys.

Expected volume is the product of all 3 stages. We simulated each promotion separately using management's planned price cuts. We then calculated gross profit for the retailer and manufacturers, assuming 20% retail and 45% manufacturer margins, under 100% and 70% pass-through of the discount.

Key findings

  • Price cuts get people to buy. A 1¢/oz category price cut raised the odds of buying detergent in a given week by 190%.
  • Promotions pull purchases forward. Households whose last purchase was on promotion were 41% less likely to buy, consistent with stocking up.
  • Displays win the brand decision. Displays raised brand choice odds by 270%, vs. 104% for a 1¢/oz price cut and 48% for feature ads.
  • Quantity is habit. How much a household bought depended almost entirely on its usual purchase size. Promotions worked by triggering purchases and brand switches, not by growing basket size.
  • Volume lift by promotion type. Displays lifted expected volume 2.9-4.2x over baseline, vs. 2.1-2.4x for feature ads and 1.7-2.3x for price cuts.
  • Profit at full pass-through. Price cuts raised retailer category profit by 20-36% and manufacturer profit by 18-31%.
  • Profit at 70% pass-through. A Tide price cut raised retailer category profit by 60% but cut Tide's manufacturer profit by 11%. For All, manufacturer profit fell 32%.

Recommendations

  1. Use full pass-through for high-demand brands like Tide, and at least 70% for lower-demand brands.
  2. Prioritize in-store displays over deep discounts. They drive the most volume without eroding margin.
  3. Apply price cuts carefully, especially at lower pass-through rates, since shoppers who buy on promotion delay their next purchase.

My role

Worked across every stage: data exploration, building and selecting the 3-stage model in SAS, the volume and profit simulations, and writing the recommendations.

Limitations and what I'd do next

  • Later reanalysisManufacturer profit was calculated brand by brand, but Tide and Cheer are both P&G brands, and Wisk and All are both Unilever. A Tide price cut pulls some sales away from Cheer. Netting that out, P&G's gain from a Tide price cut at full pass-through falls from 18.5% to about 10%. Manufacturer promotion decisions should be evaluated across the whole brand portfolio.
  • Promotion costs, such as display fees and ad spend, were not available, so the results show gross profit before promotion costs.
  • The data covers a single category, in one market, from the late 1990s, and each promotion was simulated in isolation rather than in combination.
